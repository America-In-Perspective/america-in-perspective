Why does American healthcare cost so much more?

Americans pay substantially more for healthcare than people in other wealthy nations. Understanding why takes us beyond the bills we receive and into the way healthcare is priced, insured and delivered.

1. We pay more for much of the care we receive

Research finds that higher prices for hospital and outpatient services are a major reason for the U.S. spending gap. Americans do not simply use twice as much healthcare. Many services cost more here than in comparable countries.

2. Complexity adds to the bill

American providers and insurers navigate different coverage rules, contracts, billing systems and approval procedures. A Peterson-KFF study found that administration of insurance and public health programs cost $925 per person in the United States in 2021, compared with $245 across comparable wealthy countries. That was 7.6% versus 3.8% of total health spending.

3. Other countries often negotiate prices collectively

Most wealthy nations provide , but they don't all use a single government insurer. Germany, France and Japan, for instance, have multiple insurance arrangements alongside coordinated systems for setting or negotiating many medical prices.

Think of the difference between many insurers bargaining separately with a powerful hospital and a system that establishes a shared payment schedule. Acting together—or setting regulated prices—can give purchasers greater leverage and reduce price variation. Universal coverage alone does not guarantee lower prices; the negotiating and price-setting rules matter.

4. Unaffordable care can become more serious—and more expensive

The Census Bureau estimates that 26.7 million Americans were uninsured throughout 2025. A separate Commonwealth Fund survey found that 46% of working-age adults were inadequately insured in 2026—including people who were uninsured, had gaps in coverage, or were .

Cost barriers can mean missed appointments, unfilled prescriptions and poorly controlled chronic conditions. A person who cannot afford insulin or blood-pressure medication may face complications requiring intensive treatment or hospitalization. Some of these complications can be avoided through timely, appropriate care.

Not every screening test saves money, and not every medical emergency can be prevented. But making effective routine treatment difficult to obtain can increase suffering and sometimes add avoidable costs.

5. Who pays when patients cannot?

When uninsured or underinsured Americans receive medical treatment they cannot afford, the costs don't simply disappear.

Taxpayers already help pay for this care. Federal and state governments provide billions of dollars to hospitals serving large numbers of uninsured and low-income patients. These payments help cover treatment costs that hospitals might otherwise be unable to recover.

Other costs remain with hospitals and healthcare providers, while some patients accumulate medical debt. Public financing covers part of the burden, but not every unpaid bill is reimbursed.

Could some of the money now spent treating preventable complications be used more effectively to provide affordable, timely care? Answering that question requires examining both the cost of expanded access and the savings that earlier treatment might produce.

The takeaway

U.S. healthcare spending is unusually high in large part because of medical prices and administrative complexity. Meanwhile, gaps in affordable coverage can delay appropriate treatment and sometimes lead to more serious, costly illness. Other wealthy countries demonstrate different approaches to coverage, price-setting and payment—but each approach has trade-offs.
