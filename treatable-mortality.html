Deaths from treatable conditions

Age-standardized deaths per 100,000 residents under age 75; lower is better. Norway is marked N/A because the source does not supply a comparable figure.

Why This Measure Matters

Comparing treatable mortality among wealthy democracies provides insight into how effectively healthcare systems prevent deaths that might otherwise occur without timely, high-quality medical care. This measure focuses on deaths before age 75 from conditions such as heart disease, certain cancers, diabetes, and infections that modern medicine can often successfully treat.

Because wealthy nations generally have the resources to provide advanced healthcare, differences in treatable mortality can reveal important variations in access, quality, affordability, and care coordination.

For citizens and policymakers, this measure offers a practical way to evaluate healthcare system performance and identify opportunities to save lives through more effective medical care.

Key Finding

America spends more on healthcare, yet more Americans die from treatable conditions.

According to the OECD’s Health at a Glance 2025, the United States recorded 95 deaths per 100,000 people from conditions that timely, effective medical care could potentially treat, compared with an OECD average of 77—a rate approximately 23% higher.

The contrast is even more striking when compared with Sweden, which recorded just 47 deaths per 100,000 people—less than half the American rate.

These differences raise an important question: Why does a nation that spends more per person on healthcare than any other wealthy democracy experience so many more deaths from conditions modern medicine can often treat?

Source: OECD, Health at a Glance 2025
