Life expectancy at age 65

Combined view: arithmetic mean of men’s and women’s estimates (not weighted by sex).

Why This Measure Matters

Life expectancy at age 65 offers a different perspective on longevity than life expectancy at birth. By considering only people who have reached age 65, it allows us to examine survival during later life without the influence of deaths occurring at younger ages.

Comparing this measure across wealthy democracies reveals differences in longevity among older adults and raises questions about the roles of healthcare systems, preventive care, healthy lifestyles, chronic disease management, retirement policies, and social supports for seniors.

By examining how peer nations perform, citizens and policymakers can identify approaches worthy of further investigation and consider whether their own country provides comparable conditions for a long and healthy retirement.

Scale: 17–26 additional years · 2024 estimates. Source: OECD / UN World Population Prospects.

Key Finding

America’s life expectancy disadvantage looks very different after age 65.

At birth, U.S. life expectancy trails the OECD average by 2.7 years (78.4 versus 81.1). But Americans who reach age 65 can expect approximately 20 additional years of life—nearly identical to the OECD average.

This contrast raises an interesting question: Why is America’s longevity disadvantage so much greater at birth than it is among people who have already reached age 65?

Source: OECD, Pensions at a Glance 2025 / UN WPP 2024

How to read these estimates: The OECD’s 2024 demographic table presents life expectancy at age 65 as the expected age reached (for example, 83.7 years for U.S. men). We subtract 65 to show the remaining years (18.7). These are 2024 UN population estimates used in the OECD’s Pensions at a Glance 2025, not the same observation series or year as our earlier 2023 OECD Health at a Glance life-expectancy comparison. Period life expectancy is not a prediction for any particular individual and assumes current age-specific mortality patterns. The male/female source categories are those used by the dataset.
