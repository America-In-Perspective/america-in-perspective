Healthy life expectancy: international comparison

Healthy life expectancy (HALE) estimates the equivalent number of years lived in full health, accounting for the severity of illness, injury, and disability. It does not mean people are healthy until a particular birthday and then become ill.

United States64.6equivalent healthy years
Canada67.9equivalent healthy years
Japan72.2equivalent healthy years

Scale: 60–74 equivalent healthy years · WHO, 2023.

Why This Measure Matters

Comparing healthy life expectancy among wealthy democracies provides a deeper understanding of population well-being than life expectancy alone. While life expectancy measures how long people live, healthy life expectancy estimates how many of those years are lived in good health.

The World Health Organization calculates this measure by accounting for both the duration and severity of illnesses and disabilities. For example, ten years lived with a condition that reduces health by 20% would count as eight years of full health. This doesn’t mean people suddenly become unhealthy at a particular age; it reflects the combined effects of illness and disability throughout their lives.

This distinction matters because a longer life is not necessarily a healthier one. Comparing wealthy nations raises important questions about healthcare systems, public health policies, lifestyles, environmental conditions, and social supports.

Healthy life expectancy therefore offers insight into both how long people live and the health they experience during those years.

Key Finding

Americans can expect fewer years of good health than people in the other wealthy nations we examined.

According to the World Health Organization’s 2023 estimates, healthy life expectancy in the United States was 64.6 years—the lowest among the 14 wealthy countries compared by America in Perspective. Canada reached 67.9 years, while Japan reached 72.2.

The 7.6-year gap between the United States and Japan suggests that Americans not only live shorter lives than many of their peers, but also experience substantially fewer years of good health, highlighting important differences in health outcomes across prosperous nations.
